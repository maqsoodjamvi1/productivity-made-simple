A few years ago, I felt like I was working constantly yet getting nothing important done. My days disappeared into a blur of checking email, attending meetings that could have been emails, and vaguely “working” until my eyes hurt. I kept telling myself I just needed better willpower.

The turning point came when I actually tracked my time for seven days straight. What I found shocked me: I wasn’t lacking discipline; I was leaking time in 15-minute increments. I found nearly seven hours of wandering attention, unnecessary transitions, and inefficient habits that I easily reclaimed.

If you feel like your schedule is completely full but your goals are gathering dust, you likely have the same hidden gaps. Here is how to run a time-blocking time-audit to find at least five extra hours a week without working longer days.

Step 1: Track What Actually Happens (Not What You Wish Happened)

Most people try to fix their schedules by writing an ideal daily plan. That always fails because an ideal plan assumes a friction-free reality that never exists. Instead, start by recording your actual baseline.

For three consecutive days—including at least one weekend day—write down what you are doing every 30 minutes. You don’t need fancy software for this; a simple notes app on your phone or a folded piece of paper in your pocket works fine.

Be honest. If you spent 40 minutes scrolling social media while pretending to research a project, write down “social media.” If you spent 20 minutes staring at your inbox trying to psych yourself up to reply to a difficult client, write down “email avoidance.” You cannot fix schedule leaks if you do not know where the bucket has holes.

Step 2: The Audit—Hunting for the Three Profit Killers

Once you have your raw data, grab a highlighter and look for three specific patterns that devour time without adding value:

  • Context switching penalties: Look for how often you jumped between tasks. Did you answer a Slack message, write two sentences of a report, check your phone, and then go back to the report? Every time you switch tasks, your brain takes up to 15 minutes to regain deep focus.
  • The default yes: Highlight meetings, requests, or daily habits that you only do because “that’s how we’ve always done it,” even though they no longer serve your current goals.
  • Friction gaps: Measure the dead air between tasks. How long did it take you to transition from finishing lunch to actually working? How much time vanished between the end of the workday and making dinner just because you sat on the couch?

When I did this, I realized I was losing an hour every single morning to what I call “startup friction”—drifting between coffee, news sites, and clearing notifications before doing any real work.

Step 3: Build the Blocks

Armed with your audit data, you can now construct a time-blocked schedule. Time blocking simply means assigning every hour of your working day a specific job ahead of time, rather than deciding what to do in the moment.

To find those five hidden hours, look at your audit and carve out three types of blocks:

  • Batch blocks: Instead of answering emails 47 times a day, create two 30-minute windows for inbox management. This alone usually saves an hour a day in context-switching tax.
  • Deep work blocks: Protect 90-minute windows where your phone is in another room, chat apps are closed, and you tackle your single most important project.
  • Buffer blocks: Leave 15-minute gaps between heavy meetings or tasks to decompress, stretch, or handle small logistical items, preventing your entire day from cascading into lateness.

Step 4: Protect Your System from Reality

The first week you try time blocking, it will break. A colleague will drop by your desk, a client will demand an emergency call, or you will simply underestimate how long a task takes.

When this happens, do not abandon the system. Time blocking is not a prison sentence; it is a budgeting tool. If your budget gets blown by an unexpected expense (or an unexpected meeting), you simply adjust the remaining hours of the day. The goal is not perfection, but intentionality. By forcing yourself to look at your hours as a finite resource, you stop letting other people’s urgency dictate your entire week.

Taking control of your hours starts with seeing where they actually go. Moving forward, the best next step is to map out your ideal blocks for the upcoming week and test them against your real-world constraints.
