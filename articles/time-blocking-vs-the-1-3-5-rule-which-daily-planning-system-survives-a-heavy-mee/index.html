If your calendar looks like a staircase of back-to-back Microsoft Teams or Zoom invites, you already know why most productivity advice fails. You try to build a pristine daily plan, only to watch a colleague drop a “quick sync” into your only open 45-minute window at 2:00 PM. By Thursday afternoon, your planner looks like a crime scene.

When you are drowning in meetings, your choice of daily planning framework matters immensely. Two of the most popular systems are Time Blocking and The 1-3-5 Rule. They approach the workday from opposite directions. One tries to micromanage every minute of your day, while the other focuses purely on output regardless of how chaotic your hours become.

Here is how they actually hold up when your schedule goes sideways, and how to figure out which one will keep you sane.

How Time Blocking Works (And Where It Breaks)

Time blocking is the practice of dividing your day into distinct chunks of time, assigning a specific task or category of tasks to each block. Instead of working from an endless to-do list, you look at your calendar and say, “From 9:00 to 10:30, I am writing the quarterly report. From 10:30 to 11:00, I am clearing email.”

When your calendar is empty, time blocking is a superpower. It forces you to be realistic about how long things take and stops you from endlessly bouncing between tasks.

However, time blocking requires a high degree of control over your schedule. If you have five hours of meetings locked into your calendar before noon, time blocking devolves into an administrative nightmare. You end up with jagged, two-minute time blocks between calls where you can barely log into your software, let alone do deep work. Rescheduling your blocks three times before lunch because a client ran over on a call is exhausting. Time blocking survives a heavy meeting schedule only if you are willing to fiercely guard your boundaries and ruthlessly decline or shorten meetings—a luxury many corporate employees simply do not have.

How The 1-3-5 Rule Works (And Where It Falls Short)

The 1-3-5 Rule bypasses the tyranny of the clock. Instead of scheduling every hour, you look at your day and commit to a realistic set of accomplishments. Every morning, you choose:

  • One big thing (your primary, high-impact priority that must get done)
  • Three medium things (important tasks that take moderate effort)
  • Five small things (quick maintenance tasks like approving invoices or updating a spreadsheet)

The total is nine things. The beauty of this system is its flexibility. If you get pulled into an unexpected two-hour crisis meeting from 1:00 to 3:00 PM, the 1-3-5 rule doesn’t shatter. Your schedule changed, but your targets didn’t necessarily have to. You still know that as long as you knock out your one big thing and a couple of medium items during your pockets of free time, the day was a success.

The downside? The 1-3-5 Rule assumes you have the self-discipline to hunt down your own focus time. If you lack the structural cue of a calendar block telling you when to work on that “one big thing,” it is very easy to let the entire day get swallowed up by incoming Slack messages and fire drills, leaving you scrambling to finish your medium and small tasks at 6:00 PM.

Which One Survives the Chaos?

If your job is heavy on meetings—meaning you have three or more hours of synchronous communication daily—The 1-3-5 Rule generally survives the wreckage better than strict time blocking.

When your hours are already spoken for by other people, trying to map out your own hourly schedule is an exercise in frustration. You are playing Tetris with a board that keeps changing shape. The 1-3-5 Rule keeps your eyes on the prize: completion rather than schedule adherence. It accepts the reality that your time is fragmented, challenging you to find the cracks in your day to squeeze in high-value output.

That said, the most resilient professionals often use a hybrid approach. They refuse to block out every minute of their day, but they do use time blocking for their single biggest priority.

How to Build a Hybrid System Today

If you want a system that survives a brutal calendar, stop trying to block out your whole day. Instead, try this micro-routine tomorrow morning:

  1. Write down your 1-3-5 list on a physical notepad before you open your email. Identify your one major priority.
  2. Look at your meeting-heavy calendar and find one single 45-minute or 60-minute window where you are not on camera.
  3. Drop a hard time block over only that slot, labeled strictly with your “one big thing.”
  4. Treat the rest of your day as a flexible battlefield where you check off your three medium and five small tasks whenever meetings end early or cancel.

If your calendar is too packed to even find a single 45-minute window, you have a meeting culture problem, not a productivity problem. But until you can reclaim your calendar from the status-update-call industrial complex, leaning toward a flexible outcome-based framework will save you from the constant anxiety of a broken schedule.
